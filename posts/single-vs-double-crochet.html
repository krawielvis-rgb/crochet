Materials & preparation

Before you start

Set out your yarn, hook, scissors and needle. Read the complete instructions once before making the first stitch. Write down any target stitch count or finished measurement so you can check your work as you go.

Gauge & tension

Your yarn, hook and personal tension determine the finished fabric. When size matters, make a small swatch and let it rest flat before measuring. Do not force your hands to match a number by gripping the hook harder.

Core pattern / method

Materials

  • Worsted yarn, about 40 yd / 36 m
  • 5 mm hook
  • Ruler and needle

Pattern

  1. Foundation: Ch 22.
  2. Rows 1–4: Ch 1, turn. Sc across. 21 sc each row.
  3. Rows 5–8: Ch 3, turn. Dc across. 21 dc each row.
  4. Row 9: Ch 1, turn. Sc across. 21 sc.
  5. Border: Sc around, working 2 sc in each corner. Fasten off.

What you should notice

Single crochet creates a shorter, denser fabric. Double crochet grows faster and leaves more air between stitches. Use the shorter stitch for firm fabric and the taller stitch when you want speed and drape.

Compare the two fabrics

Lay the single-crochet and double-crochet sections flat without stretching them. Compare height, thickness, visible gaps and flexibility. The stitch count should remain 21 across both sections; the difference in height should come from stitch construction, not accidental increases.

Best learning test: hold the swatch up to light, then bend it in your hand. Single crochet should look denser; double crochet should generally feel more open and flexible with the same yarn and hook.

Common sc and dc mistakes

My rows keep getting wider

Check whether you are working into a turning chain as though it were an extra stitch. Follow the pattern's instruction about whether the turning chain counts.

Double crochet is too tight

Do not pull the yarn-over snug before drawing through the loops. Keep the working loop tall enough for the hook to move smoothly through two loops at a time.

I cannot see the difference

Use a smooth, light-colored yarn and make at least four rows of each stitch. A tiny sample or fuzzy yarn can hide the change in stitch anatomy.

Finish the comparison swatch

Work the optional single-crochet border only after confirming the center sections have equal stitch counts. A border can tidy the sample, but it should not be used to disguise missing edge stitches. Weave in the tails and keep the swatch as a reference when choosing stitches for future projects.

When to choose each stitch

Single crochet is useful when you want compact fabric, such as amigurumi, sturdy pouches or dense edging. Double crochet is useful when you want faster height, more drape or a more open fabric, such as many blankets and scarves. Neither stitch is automatically better; choose based on how the finished fabric needs to behave.

Try the same sampler with a larger hook and compare how hook size changes both stitches without changing their basic structure.

Keep the swatch as a stitch reference

Label the swatch with yarn weight and hook size. It becomes a useful physical reference for how dense single crochet and double crochet feel in the same materials, especially when planning a project without a prescribed stitch.

Choose the stitch by the fabric you want

Single crochet makes a shorter, denser fabric, which is why it appears so often in amigurumi and structured pieces. Double crochet grows faster and creates a taller, more open fabric that works well for scarves, blankets, and many granny-style projects.

Neither stitch is “better.” If I want stuffing hidden, I reach for single crochet. If I want drape and faster progress, double crochet often makes more sense. Make ten stitches of each with the same yarn and hook—the difference is much clearer in your hands than in a definition.

Final notes

Use this page as a working reference: practice the technique, check the specific checkpoints above, and keep a small sample when it helps you compare future projects.
