Hey, it’s Sara

Yarn aisles can feel like a test you didn’t study for. I’m Sara — and after years of making wearables, amigurumi, and home stuff, here’s the practical version I wish someone had given me on day one.

Yarn weights that actually matter

In the US we use Craft Yarn Council categories. You don’t need to memorize every number — know these:

  • 1–2 (fingering / sport): delicate lace, light shawls, fine amigurumi details.
  • 3 (DK / light worsted): great all-rounder for garments and softer amigurumi.
  • 4 (worsted / aran): my default for beginners — blankets, scarves, most tutorials on this site.
  • 5–6 (bulky / super bulky): fast hats, chunky throws, statement pieces.

If a pattern says “worsted” and you grab bulky, your project will come out bigger and denser. Match the category first; brand second.

Fibers in plain English

Acrylic

Affordable, washable, huge color range. Perfect for practice, kids’ items, and anything that will see a washing machine. Can feel less “breathable” against skin in summer.

Cotton

Crisp stitch definition, great for bags, coasters, summer tops. Less stretch than acrylic — good for structure, less forgiving on tension mistakes.

Wool & wool blends

Warm, elastic, blocks beautifully. Check care: some are superwash, some are hand-wash only. Lovely for winter accessories.

Blends

Often the sweet spot — acrylic/wool for warmth + easy care, cotton/acrylic for bags that hold shape but don’t cost a fortune.

How I choose yarn for a project

  1. What is it for? Wearable near skin → softer fiber. Dishcloth → cotton. Kid toy → durable and washable.
  2. What does the pattern assume? Match the weight category on the pattern label.
  3. Hook pairing: worsted often likes 5.0–5.5 mm; DK 3.5–4.5 mm; bulky 6.5–9 mm. Adjust if your fabric feels stiff or holey.
  4. Swatch when size matters (garments, fitted hats). A 4×4 in (10×10 cm) square in the stitch pattern saves tears later.

Reading the label

Look for: weight category number, recommended hook, gauge (sts × rows over 4 in / 10 cm), fiber %, care symbols, and dye lot. Buy enough from the same dye lot for big projects — colors can shift between batches.

Sara’s rule of thumb: for your first ten projects, stick to worsted acrylic or cotton. Learn the stitches. Fancy yarn is more fun once the hands already know what they’re doing.

Final note from Sara

There is no morally correct yarn. There’s only “does this fabric feel right for this project?” Trust your hands.
