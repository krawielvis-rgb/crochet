Hey, it’s Sara

Patterns look like secret code until the abbreviations click. I’m Sara — this is the cheat sheet I still glance at when a designer gets creative.

These are US terms (used on this site). UK terms differ: UK dc = US sc, UK tr = US dc.

Core abbreviations

  • ch — chain
  • sl st — slip stitch
  • sc — single crochet
  • hdc — half double crochet
  • dc — double crochet
  • tr — treble (triple) crochet
  • st(s) — stitch(es)
  • sp — space
  • rep — repeat
  • rnd — round
  • RS / WS — right side / wrong side
  • FLO / BLO — front loop only / back loop only
  • inc — increase (usually 2 sts in one st)
  • dec / sc2tog — decrease (two stitches together)

Common combinations

  • sc2tog — single crochet two together (one decrease)
  • (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) — classic granny corner in one space
  • *sc, 2 sc in next*; rep around — increase round for circles
  • ch-sp — the space under a chain (not a stitch top)

How to read them in a pattern

  1. Skim the whole abbreviation list the designer provides — some use personal shortcuts.
  2. Parentheses ( ) = work all of that into the same place.
  3. Asterisks * * = repeat the section between them.
  4. Brackets often group a sequence that’s done a number of times.
Sara’s tip: keep a sticky note with sc / hdc / dc / tr until you stop thinking about it. That day comes faster than you expect.

How I read a pattern line without getting lost

I read one instruction at a time and translate punctuation into actions. Parentheses usually group stitches worked together or show the stitch count at the end. An asterisk marks a repeat. “Around” means keep repeating through the round; “across” means work to the end of a row.

For example, *sc, inc; repeat from * around (18) means single crochet once, work an increase in the next stitch, then repeat that two-stitch sequence until the round ends. The final 18 is your check. If you have 17 or 19, fix the count before moving on.

Three pattern lines, translated

Example 1: 2 sc in next st, sc in next 2 stsThis means put two single crochets into one stitch, then make one single crochet in each of the next two stitches. You used three stitches from the previous row and created four stitches.
Example 2: *dc in next 3 sts, ch 1, sk next st; rep from * acrossMake one double crochet in each of the next three stitches, chain one, skip one stitch, then start again at the asterisk. The chain creates a space while the skipped stitch keeps the repeat from simply adding width.
Example 3: (2 dc, ch 2, 2 dc) in next ch-2 spEverything inside the parentheses goes into the same chain-2 space: two double crochets, chain two, then two more double crochets. This kind of instruction is common at corners.

Abbreviations that change where the hook goes

BLO and FLO change which loop of the stitch you work under. ch-sp sends the hook into the open space made by chains instead of into the V-shaped top of a stitch. sk means skip, so do not accidentally work into the skipped stitch on the same pass.

2 sc in next st and sc2tog sound similar when you are reading quickly, but they do opposite things: the first increases the stitch count by one; the second combines two stitches into one and decreases the count by one.

US vs UK terms: the trap worth checking first

US and UK crochet use some of the same words for different stitches. In particular, US sc = UK dc, US dc = UK tr, and US tr = UK dtr. If a pattern seems dramatically taller or shorter than its photo, check the terminology before changing your tension.

When a designer supplies an abbreviation key, use that key over any generic chart. For practice reading a whole set of instructions, continue with my how to read crochet patterns guide.

Final note from Sara

You don’t need to memorize everything tonight. Learn the five you use most; the rest will follow project by project.
